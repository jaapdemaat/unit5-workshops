Information & Systems

Title: The Box That Shrank the World

Author(s): Marc Levinson

Keywords: Box, Globalisation, Standardisation, Supply chain, Labour, Automation

The shipping container revolutionised the global economy by drastically lowering shipping costs and enabling mass international trade. Introduced in 1956 by American entrepreneur Malcom McLean, containerisation replaced the slow, labour-intensive "break-bulk" method of moving individual crates. Despite early resistance from traditional ports and trade unions, international standardisation in the 1960s sparked a global boom. It transformed manufacturing, birthed complex cross-ocean supply chains, and fuelled China's economic rise. Today, containerisation underpins modern globalisation, continuously evolving through massive automated ships, specialised ports, and computer-driven logistics.

Branch points

Standardisation (the 40ft box negotiation)

What it opens onto: Systems thinking, constraint-as-enabler, grids.

New research exploration: Grid systems as agreed protocol (Josef Müller-Brockmann, Grid Systems in Graphic Design); Otl Aicher's Lufthansa/Munich '72 identity systems; ISO standards as design objects in their own right.

Invisible infrastructure

What it opens onto: What design work goes unseen vs. what gets credited.

New research exploration: Susan Leigh Star, The Ethnography of Infrastructure; Shannon Mattern's writing on urban/media infrastructure; Ingrid Burrington, Networks of New York.

McLean the outsider (trucker, not shipping man)

What it opens onto: Direct echo of Vis's Be a Stranger — good for the pre-task discussion link.

New research exploration: Outsider/naive methodology in design research; design ethnography as a way of "not knowing" a field on purpose.

Absence of an environmental/climate lens; the article never mentions carbon, fuel, or ecological cost.

What it opens onto: What is included, or excluded? Critical and closer reading/examination of the article. What are the gaps?

New research exploration: Kate Raworth, Doughnut Economics (critiques growth narratives that exclude planetary boundaries).

Time & Movement

Title: Designing for Social Change: Strategies for Community-Based Graphic Design — Chapter 1 (Immerse Yourself), Chapter 2 (Build Trust)

Author(s): Andrew Shea

Keywords: Community, Social change, Graphic Design

Author Andrew Shea presents ten strategies for successful community engagement, grounding each one in two real-world case studies. The twenty projects featured in the book are by both design professionals and students. Designing for Social Change encourages graphic designers everywhere to go out and do good with their work, providing them with the tools to complete successful projects in their communities.

In Chapter 1, the emphasis is on understanding communities, listening, building relationships, identifying stakeholders, and developing empathy before designing.

Branch points

The portrait is created by someone else, not by the subject.

What it opens onto: Questions of representation, authorship and interpretation. How do others see us? What is gained or lost when someone else tells our story?

New research exploration: Shea Ch.1: understanding community perspectives before designing. Research participatory photography, portraiture and co-authorship. References: Gillian Wearing, JR's Inside Out Project, Steve McQueen's Year 3.

Interviewing peers

What it opens onto: Design as relationship-building and listening. Community knowledge as a design resource.

New research exploration: Shea Ch.1: listening and trust building. Explore interview methods, oral histories, ethnographic observation, active listening. References: StoryCorps, Studs Terkel, social design toolkits.

Identifying shared interests, passions and experiences.

What it opens onto: Discovering common ground within a community.

New research exploration: What brings communities together? Research shared values, group identity and belonging. References: community mapping methods, social identity theory.

The project asks "Who Are We?"

What it opens onto: Defining community before designing for it.

New research exploration: What constitutes a community? How are communities formed, maintained and represented? Research communities of practice, belonging and collective identity. References: Etienne Wenger, community case studies.

Presenting members of your immediate learning community.

What it opens onto: Understanding the community you are already part of.

New research exploration: How can designers investigate familiar communities rather than distant ones? Research ethnographic observation, participant observation and self-reflexive research methods.

Strategy & Identity

Title: Making Marks (section from Caps Lock)

Author(s): Ruben Pater

Keywords: Brand design, power, symbolism, identity, capitalism

In "Making Marks" (Caps Lock, pp. 133–142), Ruben Pater traces how marks, symbols, and logos function as tools of power, identity, and control within capitalist systems. He examines how graphic marks shape perception and authority, while also highlighting their historical contingency and potential for alternative, non-corporate visual communication practices.

Branch points

Symbolism

What it opens onto: How designers represent concepts, drawing on existing patterns of visual language.

New research exploration: Visual literacy; communication theory; evolution of symbols and mark making; inscription.

Ownership

What it opens onto: Economic systems, commodification.

New research exploration: Design ethics; capitalism; cultures of extraction and exploitation; the ethical practitioner; regenerative design.

Marketing strategies

What it opens onto: Design ideas, the commercial context, persuasion.

New research exploration: Design strategies; intentionality and rationale (why); concept development and ideation; brand strategy; brand positioning.

Experience & Environment

Title: On the Passage of a Few People through a Rather Brief Moment in Time: The Situationist International 1956–1972

Author(s): Branka Bogdanov

Keywords: Situationism, Psychogeography, Dérive, Détournement, Revolt

A video documentary combining exhibition footage of the Situationist International exhibitions with film footage of the 1968 Paris student uprising, and graffiti and slogans based on the ideas of Guy Debord (one of the foremost spokesmen of the Situationist International movement). Also includes commentary by leading art critics Greil Marcus and Thomas Levine, and artists Malcolm McLaren and Jamie Reid.

Branch points

Psychogeography — see: Rebecca Solnit, Will Self & Iain Sinclair's work

What it opens onto: Fieldwork — gathering and responding to materials in the field.

New research exploration: Analysing fieldwork?

Paris '68 student uprising — see: various leftist social theories such as libertarian socialism

What it opens onto: How visual culture contributes to social movements.

New research exploration: Mapping social movements and aesthetics?

Impact on various artists/movements in the UK: street art and King Mob; Jamie Reid and punk visual culture

What it opens onto: What influenced your own references? Can you trace a line back to the Situationist International or something else? For example, you can see a clear line from the Situationists to practitioners like Fokkawolf and Led By Donkeys (graffiti/street art/political interventions through visual communications).

New research exploration: Mapping influences? What informs contemporary practice?

Narrative & Voice

Title: "My London" (article for The London Magazine)

Author(s): David Gentleman

Keywords: London, Evolution, Creativity, Observation, Energy

David Gentleman, an artist and illustrator, talks of his 70 years of living and working in London as a creative person, from his art student days to his current professional stature. Based in Camden, he has witnessed many changes in London over the years, and as an observer and visual recorder of this process of urban evolution, he has embraced these shifts as a dynamic and ongoing aspect of this capital city.

Read the source →

Branch points

Transition of London from an industrial to a post-industrial city

What it opens onto: The evolution and co-existence of different forms of architecture, co-existing from different periods.

New research exploration: Observation of the changes that have taken place in the King's Cross area — i.e. the CSM buildings, and the gasometers behind them.

Observing London from a variety of different visual points of view — on the ground, below the ground surface, and high up, looking down

What it opens onto: Different perspective points, different ways of visually depicting the "same" subject matter in dynamic and unusual ways.

New research exploration: David Gentleman's book, My Town: An Artist's Life in London, plus many other visualisations of London, coupling with the research visit to the London Museum Docklands.

Avoiding the obvious clichés, and embracing all aspects of London

What it opens onto: Identifying the clichés (i.e. Tower Bridge, the Royal Family, Beefeaters etc.) and either avoiding them completely, or re-inventing them in a fresh and unusual way. Plus incorporating less talked-about aspects of London and its history — poverty, homelessness, colonialism & the transatlantic slave trade, political protest etc.

New research exploration:

  • London: A Pilgrimage, written by Blanchard Jerrold, with 180 woodcut illustrations by Gustave Doré.
  • Brick Lane by Monica Ali — growing up in East London.
  • White Teeth by Zadie Smith — a snapshot of London in the late 1990s.
  • London: The Biography by Peter Ackroyd — treating London like a "living, breathing creature."
